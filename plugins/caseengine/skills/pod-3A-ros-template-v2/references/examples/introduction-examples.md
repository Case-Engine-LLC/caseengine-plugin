# Introduction Examples

GOOD / BAD pairs for every approved line 2 pattern in `references/introduction.md`. Read 2-3 before generating. Every example is line 2 only - line 1 is a constant and lines 3 and 4 have their own rules.

Episode used throughout: **What Your Inland Empire Car Accident Settlement Is Actually Worth**, unless the pattern needs a different topic to make sense.

---

## A - Prevalence, no number

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're getting into something a lot of people in **{{CITY}}** end up dealing with, usually with no warning, and what you actually need to know if it happens to you.

Plain, unforced, no claim that needs a source. This is the default for a reason - it never fails, it just never surprises anyone either.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're diving deep into the complex and often misunderstood world of personal injury settlements in **{{CITY}}**.

"Diving deep," "complex and often misunderstood world" - that is content-marketing voice, not speech. Nobody says "world of" out loud. It also names a practice area as a category instead of naming a thing that happens to people.

---

## B - Prevalence, with a sourced number

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. According to state crash data, roughly {{N}} people in **{{CITY}}** dealt with this in {year}, and almost none of them saw it coming. Here is what you need to know if you are one of them.

Source named in one clause, year said out loud, geography matches the data. Costs about a second and turns a claim into a fact.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Over 40,000 people are injured in car accidents in the Inland Empire every year, and most of them have no idea what their case is worth.

The number is invented, unattributed, undated, and the location is hardcoded. This is the single most dangerous failure in the whole document: it reads authoritative, an attorney will say it on air, and it traces to nothing. If research did not supply the figure, use pattern A.

### BAD - sourced but wrong

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Every year more than 38,000 people are killed on American roads, and here in **{{CITY}}** that means...

The figure is real and the source exists, but it is national data wearing a local jacket. Putting `{{CITY}}` next to a national number does not make it a local number, and "every year" hides the fact that the reader has no idea which year. Say national when it is national, or do not use it.

### BAD - stale

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. According to a 2011 study, roughly {{N}} people in **{{CITY}}** deal with this every year.

Fifteen-year-old data presented as a current annual rate. These episodes are built to rank for years, so a stale number gets more wrong every month it stays up.

## C - The local moment

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're talking about what happens when a truck comes off the 60 through Ontario at rush hour and hits somebody who was just driving home from work.

One anchor, pulled from the entity map, describing a commute a local recognizes.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're covering truck accidents on the 10, the 15, the 60, and the 91 throughout Riverside, San Bernardino, Ontario, and Fontana.

Four roads and four cities is a keyword list read aloud. It is also the exact impression the geo pairing rule exists to prevent. One anchor, or none.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're answering what people in **{{CITY}}** are searching for at two in the morning.

Searcher framing. That is how CE thinks about the audience, not how the audience thinks about itself. Name the moment, not the query.

## D - Nobody plans for this

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Today's topic is one nobody plans for. It shows up on an ordinary Tuesday in **{{CITY}}**, and what you do in the first week decides most of what comes after.

The stakes clause is a fact about timing, not a threat.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Today's topic could be the difference between getting everything you deserve and losing it all, so you cannot afford to miss this episode.

Fear pitch plus a retention beg. "Everything you deserve" and "losing it all" are both promises nobody can keep, and an attorney reading them is making a claim about outcomes.

---

## E - The misconception

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're getting into the thing almost everyone in **{{CITY}}** gets wrong about what their case is worth, and what that mistake actually costs them.

Names a mistake without naming the listener as stupid. The correction is the episode.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're debunking the top five myths about car accident settlements in **{{CITY}}**.

A listicle. It also commits the attorney to five things in an open interview with no question list, which is a promise the format cannot keep.

---

## F - The decision point

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Today is what you should know before you sign anything or agree to any number, and most people making that call have never made it before.

Anchors to a decision the listener is actually facing. Strongest for episodes aimed at people still choosing.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Today is everything you need to know before hiring a lawyer, choosing a firm, signing a fee agreement, or accepting a settlement offer in **{{CITY}}**.

Four decisions in one sentence, 30-plus words, and "everything you need to know" is a completeness claim. Pick one decision.

---

## G - Straight from the inbox

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're taking the question **{{ATTORNEY}}**'s office gets more than any other from people in **{{CITY}}**.

True and checkable - the firm knows which question that is. Makes the episode feel like it came from real people rather than a content calendar.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're answering the number one question every accident victim in **{{CITY}}** wants to know.

Same shape, no source. "Every accident victim" is a claim about a population; the GOOD version is a claim about one firm's inbox, which the firm can actually verify. Only use G when the firm confirmed the question.

---

## H - The variance

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Two people in **{{CITY}}** with the same injury can walk away with completely different numbers, and today is about why.

Sets up the whole episode in one sentence and makes "it depends" the interesting answer instead of the disappointing one.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Your case could be worth ten thousand dollars or it could be worth a million, and today we'll tell you which one you are.

Invents a range and then promises an individual valuation on a podcast. Guaranteeing a number is the red flag the attribute research explicitly names.

---

## I - The clock

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Today's topic has a deadline attached to it, and most people in **{{CITY}}** don't find out it's running until it has already run.

Conditional, same as C - only when the topic genuinely has a deadline.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**. Under the statute of limitations in **{{STATE}}**, you have two years from the date of injury to file, and today we're breaking down what that means.

Jargon in the first fifteen seconds. The substance is right; the words are a law-school outline. "One deadline ends everything, and it starts running the day it happens" says the same thing to a human.

---

## J - The other side's playbook

### GOOD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're talking about what the insurance company is doing while you're still figuring out how to get to your next appointment.

Positions the attorney against the adversary rather than against the listener's ignorance. Highest engagement in the set.

### BAD

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're exposing the dirty tricks insurance companies use to cheat victims out of what they're owed in **{{CITY}}**.

Tabloid register. It also asserts bad faith as fact, which is a thing a licensed attorney should not endorse on their own show over a topic they will next discuss professionally.

## Cross-pattern failures

These break any pattern, so they are listed once.

### BAD - re-greeting

> Welcome back to **{{PODCAST_NAME}}** with **{{ATTORNEY_NAME}}**.
> Hey everyone, welcome to the show. I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today...

Two greetings in two lines. Line 1 is the greeting.

### BAD - reading the episode title

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today we're talking about what your Inland Empire car accident settlement is actually worth.

The title is written for search. Line 2 is written for a mouth. They are allowed to share a subject, never a sentence.

### BAD - guest framing

> I'm **{{INTERVIEWER}}**, and today I'm joined by my guest **{{ATTORNEY_NAME}}** to talk about settlements in **{{CITY}}**.

It is their show. The host is the visitor.
