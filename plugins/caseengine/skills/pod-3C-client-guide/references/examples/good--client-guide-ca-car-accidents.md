## Pre-Interview Prep

### Things to Think About

- **California's pure comparative negligence system.** This is the single most important California-specific concept in the episode. Unlike states that cut off recovery at 50 or 51 percent fault, California lets someone recover damages no matter what percentage of fault is assigned to them. How would you explain that to a client sitting across from you for the first time?
- **The SR-1 form and why most people have never heard of it.** California requires drivers to file this form with the DMV within 10 days of certain accidents, and most people don't know it exists until their license gets suspended. Think about how you walk someone through this requirement.
- **What makes California car accident claims different.** The low minimum coverage limits (15/30/5), the pure comparative negligence system, the freeway volume, the government entity claims deadline. What patterns do you see in your practice that are distinctly Californian?
- **The insurance adjuster call.** Most of your clients get a call from the other driver's insurance company within days. What do you wish every person knew before picking up that phone?
- **When settlement makes sense versus going to court.** Think about the factors that tip the scale in your experience. What does the timeline look like for straightforward claims versus cases that end up in litigation in Superior Court?

### S1: At the Scene - What to Do Right Now (~10 minutes)

_This segment covers what someone should do in the immediate aftermath of a car accident in California. The goal is to give listeners a clear action plan for those chaotic first minutes - from safety and calling 911 to documenting the scene to getting medical care even when they feel fine. California-specific context matters here: the CHP handling freeway accidents, the SR-1 form requirement that most people don't know about, and the importance of getting checked out medically within that first 72-hour window._

#### Questions

- Q1: What should someone do immediately after a car accident in California to protect their claim?
  - **Move to safety first:** if the vehicles are drivable, pull to the shoulder; if not, get out of traffic and away from the lane. California Vehicle Code §20002 requires staying at the scene.
  - **Call 911 even on minor crashes:** the California Highway Patrol or local PD will run a CR-2 / Traffic Collision Report that anchors fault in the claim.
  - **Document the scene before vehicles move:** photos of every angle, the other driver's license + insurance card, license plates, weather, road conditions, witness contact info.
  - **Do not admit fault or apologize at the scene:** anything said becomes part of the claim record. Stick to facts when talking to police.
  - **Get medical evaluation within 72 hours:** soft-tissue injuries surface delayed; a gap in treatment is the adjuster's favorite tool for cutting damages.
- Q2: Should I call the police even if the accident seems minor, and what is the SR-1 form?
  - **Always call police on injury or $1000+ property damage:** California Vehicle Code §20008 requires reporting; the responding officer's CR-2 anchors fault.
  - **File the SR-1 with the California DMV within 10 days:** required on any crash with injury or $1000+ damage even if no officer responded. Missed deadline triggers license suspension.
  - **The SR-1 is not the same as the police report:** the SR-1 is your filing to the DMV; the CR-2 is the officer's report. Both matter and they capture different things.
  - **Get the report number on scene:** the responding officer will give a report number. Pull the CR-2 from the agency 7-10 days later.
- Q3: Do I need medical care even if I feel fine after the crash?
  - **Yes, within 72 hours minimum:** adrenaline masks injury at the scene; soft-tissue and concussion symptoms can take 24-72 hours to surface.
  - **Tell the provider this is a car accident:** the visit gets coded as a motor-vehicle-related medical visit, which is critical for the claim's medical-record chronology.
  - **Follow the treatment plan exactly:** missed appointments and gaps in care give the adjuster ammunition. If you stop treatment, document why.
  - **Keep every bill, EOB, and prescription receipt:** these are economic damages - they need a paper trail.

### S2: California's Insurance System and Filing the Claim (~12 minutes)

_This segment walks through the mechanics of how car accident claims actually work in California. Listeners need to understand the fault-based system, the step-by-step filing process, what documents to gather, how to handle the insurance adjuster, and the common mistakes that sink claims. The adjuster conversation and social media warnings are particularly important - most people don't realize how much their own behavior after the accident affects their recovery._

#### Questions

- Q4: How do I file a car accident insurance claim step by step, and who should I contact first?
  - **Notify your own insurer within 24 hours:** even when the other driver is clearly at fault, your policy requires prompt notice.
  - **Open the third-party liability claim with the at-fault driver's carrier:** call the carrier directly with the other driver's policy number from their insurance card.
  - **Gather the claim packet:** CR-2 police report, medical bills, photos, witness statements, repair estimates, lost-wage documentation.
  - **Get the adjuster's name, claim number, and direct line:** keep a written log of every call and what was said.
  - **Do not give a recorded statement until you have counsel:** the adjuster will ask early; the recording is used to lock in your fault percentage.
- Q5: What documents do I need to start a car accident claim?
  - **Police report (CR-2):** anchor for fault and the other driver's information.
  - **Medical records and bills:** every visit from ER through follow-up, including imaging, PT, and specialist consults.
  - **Lost-wage documentation:** pay stubs, employer statement, tax returns if self-employed.
  - **Photos of the scene, vehicles, and injuries:** taken at the scene and over the following weeks as bruising or scarring evolves.
  - **Repair estimates and out-of-pocket receipts:** rental car, tow, prescription co-pays.
- Q6: What should you say and not say to an insurance adjuster, and should you give a recorded statement?
  - **Be polite, be brief, stick to facts:** confirm date, time, location, vehicles involved. Nothing more.
  - **Never speculate about injuries or fault:** "I think I'm fine" and "I might have been going a little fast" both come back to bite you.
  - **Decline a recorded statement until counsel is retained:** California law does not require it on the at-fault driver's claim.
  - **Do not sign a medical authorization without review:** broad authorizations let the adjuster pull unrelated medical history.
- Q7: What common mistakes can hurt a car accident claim?
  - **Posting on social media:** the adjuster will find your Facebook, Instagram, and X feed. A smiling photo at a dinner cuts pain-and-suffering damages.
  - **Accepting the first offer:** opening offers are deliberately low. They expect a counter.
  - **Missing follow-up medical appointments:** every gap is treated as evidence you healed.
  - **Talking to the at-fault carrier without counsel:** they are trained to elicit fault-shifting admissions.
  - **Waiting too long to file the SR-1:** the 10-day DMV deadline runs even when the criminal investigation is open.

### S3: Fault, Evidence, and Settlement Value (~15 minutes)

_This is the longest segment and covers how fault gets assigned, what evidence drives settlement value, and how California's pure comparative negligence system works in practice. The key message is that California has no cutoff - even at 80 percent fault, a person can still recover 20 percent of their damages. Medical records, traffic camera footage, and accident reconstruction are all fair game here. This is also where hiring an attorney comes into the conversation._

#### Questions

- Q8: How is fault determined in a car accident claim, and how much weight does the police report carry?
  - **The CR-2 is the starting point, not the final word:** the officer's contributing-factor notation is the adjuster's first anchor but not legally binding.
  - **Independent evidence rebuts the officer's notation:** scene photos, dashcam, traffic camera footage, accident reconstruction.
  - **Witness statements anchor fault percentage:** independent eyewitnesses carry weight at trial.
  - **California is a pure comparative negligence state:** every driver gets a fault percentage and recovery is reduced by it - no 51% bar.
